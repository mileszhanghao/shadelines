Where the red lines were drawn, is there shade today?

In 1936 the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation graded Seattle's neighborhoods from A ("best") to D ("hazardous"), largely by who lived there. This map lays the city's 2021 tree canopy survey and today's census figures over those boundaries.

How the numbers are made

Tree canopy comes from the City of Seattle's 2016 and 2021 canopy assessment (LiDAR and aerial imagery, by census block group). Block groups don't follow 1936 boundaries, so canopy and land area are split between HOLC areas in proportion to how much of each block group they cover, then added up. Percentages are computed from those totals. Citywide, this gives 28.2% for 2021; the city's own figure is 28.1%.

People come from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey (5-year, block groups). People don't spread evenly over land, so each block group's figures are first placed onto its 2020 census blocks by where people were counted, then onto HOLC areas. Median income can't be added up; an area's figure is the average of its block groups' medians, weighted by the people it received from each, and is an approximation.

What this shows and doesn't. The 1936 grades and today's numbers line up, but a map can't separate the effect of the grades themselves from everything that happened since: highways, urban renewal, zoning, industry that was already there in 1936. The comparison is a description, not a measure of cause.
